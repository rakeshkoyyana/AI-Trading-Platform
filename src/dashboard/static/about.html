The kitchen-table desk

Every big trading firm has a floor, a research team and a budget. This one has a laptop, a free API key and a few hours after dinner.

A chart on the kitchen table

Most of AlphaWave was written between seven and eleven at night, after the real job. The day job was data engineering: moving information between systems, making it trustworthy, making it boring in the best sense. The evenings belonged to a TradingView chart that never stopped moving.

There was no desk, no quant team, no seed round. There was one person who suspected that the two halves of his life belonged together: that the discipline he used on pipelines could be pointed at a market.

A dream with a budget

It is a very middle-class kind of dream, and an honest one. Not a lottery ticket. Not a screenshot of a lucky week. The dream is that careful engineering, patience and a few good rules can turn ordinary evenings into an edge, and that someone without a trust fund or a terminal subscription can build the thing properly, by hand, and own it.

So the rules of the project were set before the first line of code: use only free tiers, keep every dollar out of the experiment, and treat money that could be lost as money that must never be risked before the machine has earned it.

Paper first. Free tools. Explain every decision.

Teaching the rules to a machine

The strategy started as a Pine Script on TradingView: a trend filter, a momentum band and a volume spike, called the triple confirmation, layered with Smart Money Concepts for context. It drew arrows on a chart. It could not place an order, keep a diary, or tell you why it stayed out of a trade.

So the script became a platform. The logic was ported to Python, checked against the chart, then wrapped with a database, a scheduler, a risk engine and a broker. One rule was never negotiable: if the system cannot explain a decision, it does not get to make it.

Earning trust, one bug at a time

The paper run began on October 2, 2026. It found its first real problems the same day. A manual close recorded no exit price. A routine check then booked the trade at its entry price. A chart that needed a refresh to show the truth. Each one was fixed before the market closed, which is the entire point of practising with play money.

What success looks like

Success is not a green number on day one. It is a system whose every line its builder understands, that survives a month of honest data, and that is allowed to risk real money only after the evidence says it has earned it. If the numbers say no, AlphaWave still did its job: it told the truth cheaply.

And if they say yes, a person with a laptop and a lamp built something that belongs to him.

  • Paper until the evidence says otherwise.
  • Nothing runs that cannot explain itself.
  • Every loss stays in the log.
  • The machine proposes; the person decides, until trust is earned.

The idea in one picture

A trade needs three things to agree, in a place on the chart where the odds are better than the crowd's.

Trend: the 9-bar average sits above the 21-bar average for a long, below it for a short. Momentum: RSI is between 50 and 70 for a long (30 to 50 for a short), so the move has room left. Volume: the bar trades more than 1.2 times its 20-bar average, so someone meaningful is participating. When all three agree on a closed 15-minute bar, there is a signal. The order goes in at the next bar's open.

One trade's life

Eleven steps between a bar closing and a trade being booked. Pick a step.

    Position size and 1:2 target

    The same arithmetic the engine uses: risk a fixed share of equity, cap the position, and set the target at twice the stop distance.

    Guardrails

    The machine is allowed to be wrong. It is not allowed to be reckless. Safety is layered, so one failure never decides the outcome.

    Gates every entry must pass

    You choose how much the machine may do, per ticker

    OffNever opens a position in this ticker. Existing positions are still managed and closed.
    AskA qualifying signal becomes a proposal with a banner and chime. Drag the stop and target, then confirm or reject. Proposals expire after 10 minutes.
    AutoA qualifying signal goes straight to the broker. Every gate and limit still applies.

    What keeps working after the order is sent

    The stop lives at the broker.

    The stop and the 1:2 target are sent with the entry and rest at Alpaca. They trigger and fill there, even if this app or your computer stops. The scheduler books the result every 30 seconds and warns once if a position ever has no resting stop.

    Your edits are checked twice.

    Dragged levels must stay on the losing side of the price, within the maximum stop width, and a stop can be widened to at most twice its original distance. The scheduler re-checks against the live price before it touches an order.

    Close and flatten.

    A Close button needs a confirm click, and a request the scheduler does not pick up within two minutes expires. Everything is flattened before the closing bell. A kill switch blocks every new order immediately.

    Experts in the shadows.

    The ML filter and a seven-voter rule council score every signal and log their opinion next to what the engine did. They cannot veto anything yet. After two to four weeks of paper trading the logs show whether either one deserves a vote.

    The road ahead

    The paper run is the experiment. Nothing gets promoted to real money, or even to a vote, until the data says so.
